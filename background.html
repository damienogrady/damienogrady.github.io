Two careers, one habit: turning complex, shifting situations into measured, tracked systems.

Measurement in the physical world

Trained in physics and applied mathematics, I spent over a decade in Hong Kong working on some of the era’s great civil engineering projects — surveying in the anchorage of the Tsing Ma Bridge, planning and temporary works drafting on the Ting Kau Bridge, Planning Engineer for internal glazing in the Hong Kong International Airport, Planning Manager for the construction of a maintenance depot for the KCRC West Rail works, and producing the Programme of Works for the Stonecutters Bridge tender for the Costain–China Harbour–JFE joint venture.

Later, in Australia, I built the system that tracked minesite steelwork from design through fabrication to installation for a major contractor — completely redeveloping the tracking database into a web-based system for minesite projects.

Ten years of construction taught me the discipline that still runs my work: if it matters, it gets measured, scheduled, and tracked. And if the measurement can’t survive scrutiny, it isn’t a measurement.

Measurement from orbit

A Masters and Ph.D. at James Cook University took me from physics and mathematics into GIS and satellite remote sensing — SAR flood-mapping research, developing radar methods for mapping water extent where clouds and darkness defeat optical satellites.

Then a decade in environmental consulting: Astron, DAS, NGIS. Technical lead over several iterations of the Fire Hazard Class mapping programme for the Queensland Fire Department. Design and development of the back end of systems measuring progress towards Sustainable Development Goals and nature- and climate-related commitments. And, alongside the archaeology community, the modelling and web construction behind the Australian ΔR Calculator — a live, public tool.

The research record is on the publications page — method development and spatial-modelling contributions to collaborative research. Commercial client work is confidential, by the client organisations’ choice, and held to the same standard.

The thread

First bridges and steelwork, now environments. The tools changed — from survey instruments and project schedules to satellites and Python pipelines — but the work has always been the same: take a situation that shifts faster than anyone can watch it, build the system that measures it, and make the numbers hold up.
